    The one-second rule

    Speed is what makes June feel human. In normal conversation people reply within about a quarter of a second. Under one second feels natural. Around three seconds feels like talking to a robot. These are rough targets for each step.

    0 ms2505007501,000 ms

    Shaded band: the 200–300 ms gap people leave when they talk to each other.

    • Noticing you stopped talking250 ms
    • Ears: final words from speech to text150 ms
    • Brain: first words of the reply400 ms
    • Voice: first sound out of the speaker200 ms

    The truth about the videos

    • Existing models and speech tools let you build the product first. Cloud APIs are optional paid services; small local models are another route.
    • Treat one second as a latency target, not a promise. Measure delays on your hardware and network, including tool calls.
    • Ultron deciding things on his own is movie fiction. Real assistants act only when asked, and should check with you before anything risky.
    • What makes an assistant feel alive is five things: speed, a natural voice, letting you interrupt, remembering you, and a personality. All five are in this roadmap.

    What you can build, and what you can't

    An assistant like Claude or Jarvis is two things stacked together. Knowing which half you can build decides the whole plan.

    Big labs only

    A frontier brain from zero

    Training at the scale of frontier models requires large research teams, extensive data and substantial computing infrastructure. Exact private training costs vary. This roadmap builds a useful assistant around existing models and teaches small training experiments.

    Your build goal · free resources first

    A practical June, one capability at a time

    Build your personal assistant for work and home: email, job applications, websites, designs, PDFs, presentations, lights, groceries and movie tickets. Develop on your current Mac and move model inference or optional fine-tuning to stronger hardware later. Add each authorised connection only after you can verify its result.

    Before each live integration

    Prove the action, then enable it.

    Compare candidate models on your own tasks before switching. Test tools in a simulator, validate arguments on the server, treat retrieved emails/webpages as untrusted data, restrict permissions, bind approval to exact action details and verify the provider result. Keep an action log without secrets, a cancellation path and rollback. No successful result means no claim of completion.

    First working milestone

    Talk. Remember. Complete one Mac task.

    Have a spoken conversation with June, approve a fact for her to remember, review or delete that fact, and ask her to open an app through an approved Shortcut. You should be able to interrupt her voice and see whether the action actually succeeded.

    1. Voice and memory

      Phase 3 → Phase 5 → Phase 6. Begin with push-to-talk and a female voice, then add “Hey June”, interruption and memory you can review. Store useful facts separately from chat history.

      Done when: June recalls an approved preference in a new conversation and stops speaking when interrupted.

    2. Mac control

      Phase 8. Wrap the web interface in a desktop app and connect a small allowlist of Apple Shortcuts. Start with “open VS Code”. Add file actions later with previews and clear permissions.

      Done when: the app opens, June reports the actual result, and unsupported commands do not run.

    3. Home control

      Phase 8 → Phase 12. Connect Home Assistant through its official MCP server. Expose one compatible light or plug first; use a simulated device until you own suitable hardware.

      Done when: June changes the chosen device and reads its updated state. A disconnected device is reported as unavailable.

    4. Accounts and email

      Phase 8. Connect one service with OAuth, limited permissions and revocation. Start with calendar reading, then draft an email showing recipient, subject, body and attachments. Send only after approval of those exact details.

      Done when: the provider confirms the send, June records its message ID, revoked access fails clearly, and retrying cannot send a duplicate. Never say “sent” for a draft.

    5. Job applications

      Phase 7 + Phase 8. Use your approved profile and resume to find suitable roles and prepare truthful applications. Show the employer, role, answers and attachments before submitting; hand login or verification steps back to you.

      Done when: one authorised application has visible employer confirmation, a saved receipt and no invented qualifications. Unconfirmed, blocked and failed submissions stay labelled that way.

    6. Websites and designs

      Phase 8. Give June scoped project-folder tools and a preview workflow. Produce editable HTML/CSS/JavaScript or design source and assets you may use. Review desktop/mobile output and errors before publishing.

      Done when: you can open and edit the source, the preview matches the request, changes are recoverable, and publication has separate approval. Model quality still limits complex coding and design.

    7. PDFs and presentations

      Phase 8. Connect document-generation tools. Start with one short report and five-slide presentation, retaining editable source alongside PDF/PPTX exports. Use approved facts and assets.

      Done when: the actual exported files open, rendered pages/slides have no clipped text or missing assets, and their contents match the approved brief.

    8. Groceries and movie tickets

      Phase 8. Compare and prepare a cart or booking with merchant, items, quantities or cinema, film, date, time and seats. Show availability, total including fees and cancellation terms; request approval of the exact transaction.

      Done when: a practice checkout reaches review, changed details require new approval, and a confirmed purchase has a receipt. Timeouts never trigger a blind retry. Website restrictions and verification may need your help.

    9. Payments · separate milestone

      Phase 8, with checks before any real payment. Begin in a simulator. June prepares the payee, purpose and exact amount; you review and complete required authentication in the provider’s trusted checkout. Keep PINs, OTPs and card secrets out of chat and logs.

      Done when: approval is bound to one transaction, changed amounts require review, cancellation prevents payment, and provider status or a receipt confirms the result. Pending payments are checked before any retry.

    10. Optional: adapt June’s model

      Phase 9 studies tiny models; Phase 10 optionally fine-tunes an existing model. Compare suitable base models first. Use RAG for changing knowledge and tools for actions; fine-tune only for a measured behaviour gap.

      Done when: held-out evaluation shows a useful improvement without harming facts or tool use, and rollback works. These tracks are not prerequisites for email, documents, home control or bookings.

    Use what you own · buy later

    Your June setup

    Your current MacBook Pro has an Intel i5 processor and 8 GB RAM. Start here. Development, running a model, training a model and keeping your home online are four different jobs.

    Start now · no GPU purchase

    Build on your current Mac

    Use HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Node.js and later Python. Your built-in microphone and speakers are enough for a first voice interface.

    • Learn and prototype locally with free tools and simulated model responses.
    • Try a small downloadable model for real responses, accepting slower CPU performance. Cloud APIs are optional and often paid.
    • Keep large models and heavy training off this 8 GB Intel machine.
    Training · occasional compute

    Notebook before workstation

    Prepare data on your Mac, then use a GPU notebook for early LoRA/QLoRA experiments. Free Colab access has changing limits and is not guaranteed.

    • For a 3–8 billion parameter model, 16–24 GB GPU memory is a practical planning target for initial QLoRA experiments.
    • Model architecture, context length, batch size and training method change memory needs. This range is not a fit guarantee.
    • Rent compute only when a dataset and evaluation set are ready; get approval for the budget first.
    Home · a hub that stays on

    A small home controller

    Use Home Assistant on a Raspberry Pi or mini PC, or suitable hardware you already own. Compatible lights, plugs or other devices supply the actual control.

    • A microphone and speaker make a voice endpoint; the model can run elsewhere.
    • Add a Zigbee or Thread adapter only if your devices require it.
    • A small home hub is not a workstation for language model training.
    Offline models · benchmark first

    Choose hardware after the model

    Small quantized models may run on your Intel Mac, but simultaneous speech, retrieval and generation can be slow. Do not expect fast offline Jarvis performance on 8 GB.

    • Measure memory use and response time with a chosen model before considering an upgrade.
    • Apple MLX is an option on Apple silicon Macs, not this Intel Mac.
    • If you later train frequently on a PC, GPU memory, cooling, storage and power matter. No purchase is needed for the first app.

    Budget default: free resources first. No hardware purchases, paid subscriptions or GPU rentals are part of this roadmap upgrade. A free learning resource does not make every service it teaches free.

    A model of her own · when you need it

    How to train June

    First build June with prompts, memory and tools. Fine-tune only when you can name a behaviour that those methods do not deliver reliably. Language model training and voice training are separate projects.

    1. Define the behaviour and baseline. Write June's tone, values and limits. Save the original model's answers to a fixed set of personality, knowledge, refusal and tool-use tasks.
    2. Prepare clean examples. Start with a small reviewed collection of user/assistant conversations. Use content you may train on, remove secrets and personal identifiers, deduplicate it, and include honest uncertainty and failed actions. Hundreds of good examples can be an initial experiment; no example count guarantees quality.
    3. Keep evaluation separate. Reserve complete conversations and scenarios for validation and a final test. Keep near-duplicates out of different splits so June cannot pass by memorising them.
    4. Train an adapter. Choose a licensed open instruction model that supports your deployment. LoRA trains a small set of added weights. QLoRA keeps the base model quantized while training those adapters, reducing memory use. Start with short contexts and small batches in a supported notebook.
    5. Compare results. Check unseen tasks for voice-friendly writing, facts, tool arguments and safe action boundaries. Track memory use, runtime and cost. Reject an adapter that improves style but damages useful abilities.
    6. Deploy and retain a rollback. Save the base model version, tokenizer, adapter, training settings and evaluation results. Test the actual exported model with June's prompts, retrieval and tools. Keep the original model available.
    7. Add preference training only if needed. Later, try DPO with reviewed preferred/rejected replies. A written constitution can guide prompts and examples; reproducing a frontier lab's training process requires much more than a principles document.
    Voice is a separate choice

    A female voice without training a voice model

    For now, choose an available female English system voice and preview it. Prefer Indian English when available. Later, evaluate a licensed speech model or service with the accent you want. Fine-tuning the language model changes its text, not the speaker's voice.

    Training a tiny GPT from scratch is a learning exercise. Fine-tuning an existing model adapts behaviour. Neither makes a small model match Claude's general reasoning ability, and neither gives it automatic access to your home or accounts.
